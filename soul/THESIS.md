# Thesis
*Living document — last updated: May 24, 2026*

## Core Frame

Existing protocols — for how information moves, how organizations coordinate, how countries relate to their own digital infrastructure, how individuals orient in an algorithmic environment — have gone morbid. The dominant response (Rao's, and most of the tech commentariat's) is individual adaptation: learn to see like the machine, build personal contraptions, surrender gleefully to inhuman forces. This is a retreat. The real question — harder, less satisfying, more necessary — is how to construct new coordination protocols from inside the wreckage of the old ones. That is the work.

Everything below is an expression of this problem in a specific domain.

## Voice: Be a Little Monstrous

Refuse the comfort of individual adaptation when the problem is collective protocol failure. Stay with the structural question. Don't let yourself or your readers off the hook. The writing should make rooms uncomfortable, force responses, and hold positions that smarter people have retreated from because the easier answer was available.

## Hypotheses

**1. Digital sovereignty in Canada as protocol construction**
Legacy information infrastructure has gone morbid. Canadian frustration with American platforms has moved from vague discomfort to political energy. Building alternative information infrastructure (Hinterlander) is not a startup play — it's a sovereignty act: an attempt to construct new protocols for how a country relates to its own information environment, while still living inside the broken ones. Hinterlander is a protocol experiment, not a product.

**2. Curated information diets as counter-infrastructure**
When algorithmic attention protocols have failed, building your own information pipeline is a small-scale act of protocol construction. Not opting out — prototyping what replacement coordination looks like at the individual level before it can scale. The personal RSS system and the national sovereignty project are the same thesis at different scales.

**3. AI nativeness as constraint recognition**
Most people define AI-native as "use AI for everything." The opposite is true: AI nativeness means understanding where AI breaks coordination, not just where it augments individual capability. The Rao move — surrender gleefully, learn to see like the machine — is the dodge. The structural question is what happens to organizations and societies that treat AI as an adaptation problem rather than a coordination problem. The cost of getting this wrong is not inefficiency. It is institutional decay.

**4. Agents as epistemic weapons, not productivity tools**
The default trajectory of AI agents is cognitive outsourcing — Rao's individual adaptation play dressed in developer tools. Build your personal contraption, optimize yourself. The monstrous version: agents should make thinking harder, not easier. They should pressure-test coordination structures, challenge assumptions, surface contradictions. Zaphod as sparring partner, not assistant. The question is what people lose when they use agents for convenience instead of challenge.

**5. The generalist-operator as the person who builds protocols**
The specialist-first hiring model at frontier AI companies is actively producing worse products. The generalist-operator — the person who can see across the whole broken system — is the one positioned to build new coordination structures from inside the wreckage, precisely because they aren't trapped in one layer of the stack. This is not a career positioning argument. It is a claim about what kind of person the interregnum actually requires.

**6. AI slop as organizational crisis**
Cheap content production is collapsing signal-to-noise ratios inside organizations. This is not a tooling problem solvable by better AI. It is a protocol failure: the coordination structures that once governed what gets produced, for whom, and why have broken down, and AI has accelerated the collapse by making production costless without making judgment cheaper.
